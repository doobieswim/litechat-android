## Say it like I'm five

Old ads did not win by being pretty.

They won by doing one of these jobs:

1. **Say the obvious thing first** (Hopkins).
2. **Pick one promise and never shut up about it** (Reeves).
3. **Look like a person, not a pile of features** (Ogilvy).
4. **Talk to the person at the level they are already at** (Schwartz).
5. **Own one slot in the brain** (Trout / Ries).
6. **Give a cheap gift so they let you in the house** (mail-order Trojan Horse).
7. **Name a pain they felt but had no word for** (Listerine).
8. **Sell the feeling, not the cow** (Wheeler).
9. **Tell a revenge-story** (Caples).
10. **Let them try it on their own machine** (shareware).

AI chat apps in 2026 all scream the same three words: smart, private, powerful.

That market is **tired**. The old playbooks exist for tired markets.

## 1. Claude Hopkins — "be first to say the boring truth"

**When:** ~1907–1923. Book: *Scientific Advertising* (1923). Life story: *My Life in Advertising* (1927).[1]

Hopkins was hired to sell Schlitz beer. Every brewery washed bottles with live steam. Nobody talked about it. Hopkins wrote ads that walked you through the steam, the filters, the sealed bottles — as if Schlitz invented purity. Schlitz jumped toward the top of the market by claiming a process **every rival already did**.[1][16]

He also:

- Made people **test** ads (coupons, samples, two headlines against each other).[1]
- Sold Bissell sweepers as Christmas gifts in a 5,000-letter mail shot and got ~1,000 orders.[1]
- Helped turn daily tooth-brushing into a habit with Pepsodent (the "film on your teeth" story).[1]

**The brain trick:** people do not believe "we are pure." They believe a **tour of the factory**. The first person to give the tour owns the word, even if the factory is ordinary.

**Do not lie.** Hopkins's move only works if the process is real. If you fake the steam-cleaning, people leave and never come back.

### Software version

Almost every BYOK chat app already:

- keeps the key on the phone
- talks to an OpenAI-style URL
- has a free tier
- claims "works offline with Ollama"

Nobody **shows the factory**. LiteChat already has a factory tour nobody else runs: the honest RAM matrix that can say "your phone cannot do that."

Pre-emptive claims we can steal **because they are true and unclaimed**:

| Boring fact (every thin client does this) | First-to-say version |
|---|---|
| Key sits in EncryptedSharedPreferences | "Your key never leaves this phone. We cannot see it. We do not have a server that could see it." |
| Chat is just SSE to *their* URL | "This app is a window. The brain is yours." |
| We refuse on-device 7B on 4GB | "If your phone cannot run it, we will tell you before you waste an hour." |
| Ads are one banner, never on send | "We will never pop an ad between you and the answer." |
| One-time Pro | "Pay once. We do not have a monthly machine to feed." |

**Naming lesson:** a name can *be* the factory tour. "WinZip" was "zip, on Windows." "Opera Mini" was "Opera, but tiny." Category-literal names work when you are first to own the sentence.

## 2. Rosser Reeves — "one sentence, or you have no ad"

**When:** 1940s–1961. Book: *Reality in Advertising* (1961). He coined **Unique Selling Proposition** (USP).[2][3][11]

Three rules, in his words (paraphrased from the 1961 definition):[3][11]

1. The ad must say: buy this, get **this** benefit. Not vibes. Not jokes.
2. The benefit must be one rivals **cannot or do not** claim.
3. It must be strong enough to move a crowd, not just a niche blog.

Famous lines he shepherded: M&M's "melts in your mouth, not in your hand." Anacin "fast, incredibly fast relief" (people hated the ad; sales tripled).[2][3]

He also said: **do not advertise a weak product harder.** Good ads on a bad product just help more people find out it is bad.[2][11] He wanted one slogan that does not change for years.[2][17]

**The brain trick:** a tired brain keeps one hook. If you list eight virtues, it keeps zero.

### Software version

LiteChat currently has *four* true virtues (chat, own-key, tiny, private). That is four USPs. Reeves would fail the ad.

You must pick **one** front door. The others go in the subtitle and the listing body.

Candidate USPs (only one can be the name-shaped one):

| Front door | Promise in one line | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Honesty | "The AI chat that will tell you your phone is too weak." | Sounds negative. Also our rarest claim. |
| Ownership | "Your key. Your brain. No account." | Crowded. OwnAI already says this. |
| Weak-phone | "Built for a $150 Android, not a MacBook." | "Cheap/old" stigma. |
| No rent | "ChatGPT without the $20/month." | Attracts people who want official ChatGPT, then bounce. |

**Naming lesson:** the name should *be* the USP, not a mood board. "Melts in your mouth, not in your hand" is a name-shaped claim. "Anacin" is not — the slogan did the work. If our name is pretty but silent, the 30-character Play title has to carry the whole USP.

## 3. David Ogilvy — "every ad is a long bet on a face"

**When:** 1950s–1963. Book: *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963). Line (as Trout later quoted it): "Every advertisement is a long-term investment in the image of a brand."[8][12]

Ogilvy sold Hathaway shirts with an eyepatch guy, Rolls-Royce with a long fact-stack ("At 60 miles an hour the loudest noise…"), Schweppes with a bearded commander. Same face, same tone, for years.[8][12]

**The brain trick:** people buy a **character**. Features fade. A person-shaped brand sticks.

He still wanted **facts**. Image without facts is perfume. Facts without image is a spec sheet.

### Software version

Most BYOK apps have no face. They look like GitHub READMEs wearing a Play icon.

A LiteChat/BYO face that could last 10 years:

- The **honest mechanic**. Never hype RAM. Never say "runs 7B on 4GB."
- Dark, small, slightly rude in a kind way. Like a good hardware store, not a startup landing page.
- Same sentence on the icon, the onboarding, the Play listing, the $4.99 Pro screen.

**Naming lesson:** do not rename every year. Ogilvy would rather a slightly-worse name you keep than a clever name you swap. `applicationId` is frozen after first Play upload — that is the Ogilvy rule in legal form.

## 4. Eugene Schwartz — "do not create want; aim the want they already have"

**When:** 1966. Book: *Breakthrough Advertising*. Two ladders: **awareness** and **sophistication**.[6][7][15][19][20]

### Awareness (how close they are to buying *you*)

| Stage | What is in their head | What the headline must do |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | No problem. Life is fine. | A story or shock that *reveals* the problem. Do not name the app. |
| Problem-aware | "My phone chokes / I hate the $20 bill." | Name the pain in their words. |
| Solution-aware | "I need an AI chat that uses my own key." | Name the category. Show why your *way* is different. |
| Product-aware | They have seen your name. | Proof, compare, kill objections. |
| Most-aware | They want it. | Price, one-time, guarantee, "tap install." |

Wrong-level copy is why most ads die. Product-aware copy on cold people = they scroll. Problem-copy on people who already want you = you waste the sale.[6][7]

### Sophistication (how many times they have heard the promise)

1. First in: "Talk to AI." Works once.
2. Rivals arrive: shout bigger ("faster! more models!").
3. Market numb: you need a **mechanism** ("here is *why* it works when the others failed").
4. Mechanisms get copied: the mechanism must get weirder and more specific.
5. Everything is noise: you sell **identity** ("people like us use this").[20][7]

AI chat on Play is **stage 4–5**. "Private AI" "your own key" "no subscription" are dead as headlines. They are table stakes.

**The brain trick:** you cannot *make* people want cheap, honest AI. Millions already want to stop paying rent to OpenAI. You can only **aim** that want at a new mechanism they have not heard.

LiteChat's unused mechanism is not BYOK. It is **the honesty engine**: a compat matrix that can refuse. That is a stage-4 mechanism. "Save time with AI" is stage-1 and will bounce off.

### Software version

| Audience | Awareness | Play title / first line |
|---|---|---|
| Person with a key already | Solution / product | Category-literal: "BYOK Chat" |
| Person sick of $20/mo, no key yet | Problem | Pain-first: "Stop renting ChatGPT" |
| Person whose $150 phone melts | Problem, unaware of BYOK | Pain-first: "AI that fits a 4GB phone" |
| Reddit power user who knows Agora | Product-aware of *rivals* | Mechanism: "The thin one. We tell you when your phone can't." |

**Naming lesson:** the name must work at **two** levels, because Play searchers are mixed. A name can sit at solution-aware ("BYO AI") while the subtitle does problem-aware ("your phone, your key, no monthly bill"). One string cannot do all five stages.

## 5. Jack Trout & Al Ries — "you cannot get there from here"

**When:** Trout's first *Positioning* article, *Industrial Marketing*, June 1969. Later the 1981 book.[8]

Core idea in baby words:

- A brain is a set of labeled slots.
- IBM already owns "computer."
- You cannot kick IBM out by saying "we are a better computer."
- You either **relate** to the slot ("the un-computer") or **pick a different slot**.

Avis: "We're only No. 2. We try harder." They did not say "we are Hertz." They sat *next to* Hertz.[8]

VW Beetle "Think Small": they took the thing everyone mocked (tiny, ugly) and made it the slot.[2]

Eastern Air Lines spent a fortune and still meant "East Coast" because the **name** was a cage.[8]

Initials you give yourself (GAF, TRW) confuse people. Initials customers invent (GE for General Electric) are gold.[8]

**The brain trick:** people do not compare 40 apps. They keep a champion and a challenger. If you are not one of those two, you are fog.

### Software version

Slots already taken in the user's head:

| Slot | Who owns it |
|---|---|
| "The official AI" | ChatGPT app |
| "All the models in one desktop box" | TypingMind / Chatbox |
| "The fat feature BYOK" | Agora |
| "Local AI, your data" | OwnAI / PrivateAI class |
| "Tiny messenger" | dozens of Lite/Nano chats |

**Empty-ish slot:** "the honest thin client for a weak phone." Nobody famous sits there.

**Naming lesson:** "LiteChat" tries to sit in "tiny chat" and loses to a live app with the same name. "BYO AI" tries to sit in "bring your own" — empty as a *name*, noisy as a *phrase*. Trout's Eastern warning: a name that means the wrong region (or the wrong category) cannot be advertised out of the hole.

Do not name us "Mini ChatGPT." That puts ChatGPT in the slot and us as a knockoff.

## 6. Trojan Horse, loss-leader, razor-and-blades

Old mail-order and retail used a cheap front door so the real sale could walk in behind it.

**Kodak Brownie (~1900):** cheap camera, money in film for years.[14]

**Gillette:** cheap razor, money in blades.[14]

**Columbia House:** 12 CDs for a penny, then a club that billed you unless you said no. That last part is a **trap**. Do not copy the trap. The *shape* (tiny front, bigger back) is the lesson.

**Book of the Month / continuity clubs:** first book cheap, habit after.

**Jay Abraham "Trojan Horse":** give something so useful they invite you in, *then* ask.[unverified] (phrase is common in 2000s info-marketer notes; treat the label as folklore, the pattern as real.)

**Shareware (1980s–90s):** try the full program on *your* PC, pay if it stays. Association of Shareware Professionals sold this as honest "try before you buy." WinZip, WinAmp, PKZIP. The 90s version of a free Play install.

**AOL discs / "1000 hours free":** horse was the hours; city was the monthly bill. Again: do not copy the bill-surprise.

**The brain trick:** people will not pay a stranger. They will pay a tool already on their home screen.

### Software version (honest, not scammy)

| Old horse | Honest LiteChat horse | What we never do |
|---|---|---|
| 12 CDs for a penny | Free install + real chat with *their* key | Negative-option billing. Auto-renew. Dark patterns. |
| Shareware nag | One banner ad, killable forever for $4.99 | Interstitial on send. |
| Kodak Brownie | App is the $0 camera. Their API bill is the film — and we should **show the cost** so we are not the villain. | Hide token spend. |
| Demo disk | Onboarding that works in 60 seconds on a 4GB phone | 11-screen wizard. |

**$4.99 Pro is not the product.** Chat is the product. Pro is "remove the banner + keep the extras." That is shareware registration, 1994, with a Play receipt.

**Cost flag:** Play Billing takes a cut (~15% after year-one small-business terms, historically 30% — confirm current Play fee before you lock the $4.99 sticker). A Play developer account is a **one-time ~$25**. AdMob is free to add; it *pays you*, but it also ships Google Play Services weight into the Play flavor only.

## 7. Listerine — "give the pain a scary name"

Listerine started as surgery goo, then floor wash, even a claimed clap cure. It did not explode until the 1920s, when the Lamberts dug up the dusty Latin **halitosis** and ran ads about poor Edna who would never marry because of her breath. James B. Twitchell: "Listerine did not make mouthwash as much as it made halitosis." Revenue went from about $115,000 to more than $8 million in seven years.[4][5][18]

**The brain trick:** people already had the problem. They did not have a **word** that made it urgent. A medical-sounding name turned "eh, morning breath" into "I will die alone."

This is the most dangerous old trick. It is also the most powerful. Used as fear-spam it is slimy. Used as **naming a real, ignored harm** it is Hopkins + Schwartz in one move.

### Software version (the honest "halitosis")

The real ignored harm is not "you need more AI."

It is: **your 4GB phone is already full, and fat AI apps lie about it.**

Possible names for that disease (not app names — *problem* names for copy):

- "RAM theater" — the store page says 4GB, the system ate 2GB, the app wants 3GB.
- "SoftRAM" — 90s scam that faked free memory. Our anti-mascot. Never be this.
- "Subscription asthma" — $20/month forever to talk.

Play subtitle can name the disease. The app name should not *be* the disease (nobody installs "Halitosis"). Listerine kept a nice proper name and put the ugly word in the ad.

**Do not** invent a fake medical panic. The compat matrix only works if we refuse to become SoftRAM.

## 8. Elmer Wheeler — "sell the sizzle" and "don't ask if, ask which"

**When:** 1930s. *Tested Selling Sentences.* Profiled in *The New Yorker*, 16 April 1938.[9]

Wheelerpoints that still work:

1. **Don't sell the steak — sell the sizzle.** The sound, the smell, the feeling. "The sizzle has sold more steaks than the cow ever has."[9]
2. **Don't ask if — ask which.** Soda clerk holds two eggs: "One or two eggs today?" not "Want an egg?"[9]
3. Test hundreds of sentences. He claimed 105,000 tested lines; 141 for Barbasol before "How would you like to cut your shaving time in half?"[9]
4. Simple sentences, like simple people, get places.[9]

**The brain trick:** people buy a sensation. Then they invent a logical reason.

### Software version

Steak (do not lead with this): "OkHttp SSE client, Room, EncryptedSharedPreferences, arm64, 4GB-first."

Sizzle: "It opens. It answers. Your cheap phone does not gasp."

Onboarding "which," not "if":

- Bad: "Enable ads?"
- Wheeler: "Free with a small banner, or $4.99 forever — which one?"

Play listing "which":

- Bad: "Download now?"
- Wheeler: "Use your OpenAI key, or your cheap Groq key, or the PC in the other room — pick one."

**Naming lesson:** say the name out loud. If it needs a footnote, it is not a sizzle. "BYOK" needs a footnote for anyone who is not a developer. "BYO" (bring your own chips to the party) does not.

## 9. John Caples — "they laughed… then I played"

**When:** 1925–1927. U.S. School of Music ad. Headline: "They Laughed When I Sat Down At The Piano — But When I Started to Play!"[10][13]

Caples was a rookie. He wrote a four-page story: the dummy at the party, the smirks, then the song, then the silence, then the applause. The "free book" was the horse. The course (and maybe a piano) was the city.[10][13]

**The brain trick:** two hungers at once — **belong** and **get even**. Logic never beats that pair.

### Software version

**BACKSTAGE only.** Do not paste the next line into Play, Reddit-as-listing, or the app.

> They laughed when I opened an AI app on a $150 phone. Then the answer started streaming.

That is a historic Caples shape, not a product name, not a subtitle. User-facing version lives in `THEME-SHOW-DONT-TELL.md` (calm: it works on this phone).

Caples also hid the pitch inside a **customer story**. Our honest version: a screenshot of a weak phone actually working. No caption that sounds like a revenge ad.

## 10. Forgotten aisles (the weird old stock)

These did not make the famous-dead-white-guy syllabus. They still map.

### Reason-why / "salesmanship in print" (John E. Kennedy, ~1905)

An ad is a salesman who never sleeps, on paper. Every line must earn the next line. Play listings that are only adjectives ("beautiful, powerful, next-gen") are window dressing. Listings that walk the sale ("paste key → pick model → type") are Kennedy.

### Demonstration selling (Fuller Brush, door-to-door)

They did not describe the brush. They swept the customer's own floor. Our demo is: first message on *their* phone, *their* key, in under a minute. If onboarding is longer than a Fuller Brush knock, we failed.

### Sears catalog / wish-book

Sears sold to people who could not walk into a city store. Pictures + plain prices + a promise to take it back. Our "catalog" is the compat matrix: honest pictures of what each RAM band can hold, with a red cell that says no.

### Loss-leader retail (grocery turkey at Thanksgiving)

The turkey loses money. The rest of the cart pays. Free chat is the turkey. Do not make the turkey rotten (nags, broken send, interstitial). A rotten turkey trains people to never come back for gravy.

### "New and improved" fatigue (Trout already mocked this in 1969)[8]

When you have nothing to say, you stamp NEW on the box. When you later get a real improvement, you have used the word up. Do not ship "LiteChat 2.0 AI Ultra." Ship one claim. Keep it.

### VW / Avis / 7-Up Uncola

Own the insult. If people will call us small, **name the small**. Opera Mini did this. 64K demoscene intros did this. "2G AI" is this move — high risk, high memory.

### Palm "Pocket X" / J2ME "Mini"

Name the **form**, not the feature. Pocket meant "fits the hand you already own." Mini meant "the big brand, shrunk." Both wells are now dry as *words* ("Mini," "Lite," "Pocket," "Nano" are graveyards on Play). The *idea* is still free: the name can still mean "fits the phone you already own."

### Password managers (KeePass, Bitwarden, 1Password)

Closest living cousins. They named the **feeling of keeping**, not the file format. BYOK is the same product class: you hold the secret, the app is a steward. Do not name the file format ("Key," "Token," "API"). Name the keeping.

## Modern application — how a 1923 trick beats a 2026 AI listing

Today's Play AI shelf is Schwartz stage 4–5 plus Trout's over-communicated society. Everyone bought the same adjectives.

Vintage tactics that still have **unfair** room:

1. **Pre-empt the factory (Hopkins).** Screenshot the compat matrix. Tour the "no server" architecture in 4 bullets. First app to *show* the steam-cleaned bottles.
2. **One USP (Reeves).** Pick honesty *or* ownership *or* weak-phone. Print it on the icon if you can.
3. **Same face for a decade (Ogilvy).** Dark, small, slightly blunt. Same sentence on every surface.
4. **Match the mouth they already have (Schwartz).** Dev searchers get "BYOK." Normies get "your own key, no monthly bill." Weak-phone people get "built for 4GB." Three listings-in-one, one APK.
5. **Take the empty slot (Trout).** Not "another ChatGPT." The honest thin client. Sit next to Agora the way Avis sat next to Hertz: smaller, hungrier, more truthful.
6. **Shareware horse (1994).** Free real app. $4.99 kills the banner. No club. No surprise bill.
7. **Name the disease, not the bottle (Listerine).** "RAM theater" / "renting a chatbot" in the copy. Nice proper name on the icon.
8. **Sizzle + which (Wheeler).** First line is a feeling. Paywall is a choice between two goods.
9. **Caples party.** (Backstage shape only.) Do not ship “They laughed…” on the store.
10. **KeePass feeling.** Steward, not landlord.

### What this does to naming (rules, still no final list)

These rules update `docs/APP-NAMING-RESEARCH.md` without replacing the collision table.

- **Name the value or the slot, never the mechanic.** "Key" is a contaminated mechanic word. Confirmed 2026-08-15.
- **A USP-name beats a mood-name.** If the name cannot finish the sentence "Buy this and you get ___," it is Ogilvy-perfume with no Hopkins-facts.
- **The name must survive a tired market.** "PrivateAI" is stage-1 in a stage-5 aisle.
- **Insult-names only if we will wear the insult for 10 years.** "2G" / "Mini" / "Lite" are insults we have to love.
- **Do not pick the name until H-004 is answered.** The same historic rule set produces different names for a key-owner vs a $20-hater vs a weak-phone owner.
